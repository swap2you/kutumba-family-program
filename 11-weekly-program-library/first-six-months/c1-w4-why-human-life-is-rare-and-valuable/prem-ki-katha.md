## 6. Source-grounded narrative

_[Source narrative / paraphrase — not invented dialogue]_

Caitanya-caritāmṛta, Madhya-līlā 24.229–282 records Nārada Muni meeting a hunter who left animals wounded and suffering. Nārada asked why he did not kill them outright instead of leaving them in pain. The hunter identified himself as Mṛgāri and explained that his father had taught him this practice (CC Madhya 24.242). The account shows human intelligence being misused, but it also shows that a person shaped by harmful training can hear, reconsider, and change.

Nārada explained the consequences of causing such suffering. Mṛgāri became afraid and asked how he could be freed from his sinful reactions. Nārada told him first to break his bow. Although the hunter worried about supporting himself, he trusted Nārada's assurance and broke it (CC Madhya 24.255–256). The broken bow is the story's visible turning point: instruction was followed by a concrete change in conduct.

Nārada then gave practical devotional direction. Mṛgāri distributed what he had, lived simply near a tulasī plant, worshiped and circumambulated her, and chanted the holy name. Nārada arranged that food would be supplied, and villagers began bringing provisions. Mṛgāri accepted only what he and his wife needed. This part of the account joins moral correction with support and sustainable practice.

When Nārada later returned with Parvata Muni, Mṛgāri carefully moved ants from his path with his cloth before approaching them (CC Madhya 24.270–272). The man who had once caused suffering now took care not to injure even the smallest creatures. In the concluding verses, Parvata Muni praises Nārada's transforming association, and Mṛgāri receives the Vaiṣṇavas with devotion (CC Madhya 24.278–282).

This kathā supports the week's primary verse, Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.9.29: human life is rare, temporary, and capable of the highest value. Mṛgāri's change does not come from comfort or status. It begins when he hears authoritative instruction, accepts responsibility, and redirects his abilities toward devotional practice. Human life is valuable because informed reform and devotional commitment are possible.

For families, the application must remain proportionate. A broken bow is not a demand for dramatic gestures; it is a prompt to identify one harmful habit and take one real step away from it. Adults can pair correction with practical support. Youth can ask which inherited habits deserve examination. Younger children can remember the simple, source-grounded arc: the hunter stopped hurting animals, followed Nārada's instruction, chanted, and learned to protect living beings.

The kathā avoids graphic detail, invented dialogue, and claims not present in CC Madhya 24. Its central sequence is enough: Nārada sees suffering, questions Mṛgāri, teaches consequences, asks him to break the bow, gives devotional instruction and support, and later witnesses his careful compassion.

**Source-bounded expansion — the first encounter (CC Madhya 24.229–241).** The account begins by directing attention to suffering that Nārada did not ignore. He saw wounded animals and responded with compassion and inquiry. When the animals fled at Nārada's arrival, the hunter objected because his hunt had been interrupted. Nārada did not answer with violence. He asked who had left the animals in that condition and why they had been made to suffer in that way. This opening matters because the change begins with truthful observation. Harm is neither hidden nor described for spectacle. A facilitator can narrate only that the animals were wounded and distressed; younger listeners do not need further detail.

**Source-bounded expansion — name and inherited training (CC Madhya 24.242).** Mṛgāri identifies the practice as one learned from his father. This fact explains how cruelty had become normal to him; it does not excuse the cruelty. The distinction is useful in family education. Children and adults inherit patterns of speech, consumption, anger, and avoidance. Naming where a pattern came from can help someone understand it, while responsibility still requires a different choice once the harm is recognized.

**Source-bounded expansion — consequences and surrender (CC Madhya 24.243–256).** Nārada explains that deliberately prolonging the animals' suffering brings grave reactions. Mṛgāri becomes afraid and asks for a path of release. The emotional movement belongs to the source: confidence in the old practice gives way to concern, inquiry, and willingness to follow instruction. The narration need not turn those consequences into threats aimed at children. The purpose is to show moral causality and the possibility of accepting correction.

Nārada then asks for a specific act: break the bow. Mṛgāri's concern about livelihood is practical rather than abstract. Stopping a harmful pattern may create a real gap in routine, income, identity, or belonging. Nārada does not dismiss that concern. He assures Mṛgāri that necessities will be arranged. Only then does Mṛgāri break the bow and surrender to the instruction (CC Madhya 24.255–256). The source therefore supports a careful family principle: truthful correction should be joined with realistic help wherever change carries practical cost.

**Source-bounded expansion — a new devotional pattern (CC Madhya 24.257–269).** After the bow is broken, Nārada does not leave Mṛgāri with only a prohibition. He gives positive practices and a simple way of life. The hunter is directed to distribute his accumulated goods, keep only basic clothing for himself and his wife, live near a tulasī plant, worship and circumambulate her, and chant the holy name. Food is arranged through the community. When provisions arrive in abundance, Mṛgāri and his wife take only what they require.

This sequence gives the broken bow its full meaning. Renunciation is followed by a devotional center, daily practice, community support, and restraint. The story is not merely “stop doing something bad.” It is “hear authorized guidance, stop the harm, and organize life around service and the holy name.” Human life becomes valuable through positive direction. A vacant habit can return; a supported devotional pattern gives the person somewhere truthful to stand.

**Source-bounded expansion — careful steps around ants (CC Madhya 24.270–272).** When Nārada and Parvata Muni later approach, Mṛgāri wants to welcome them. He notices ants in the way and uses his cloth to move them carefully before offering respect. This is the verified image for younger narration. The text does not require a sideways walk, mockery from outsiders, or an explanation from Nārada to a laughing crowd. Those details are omitted because they are not needed to show the change.

The contrast is carried by conduct. Earlier, Mṛgāri prolonged suffering; now he slows down to avoid harming ants. His compassion is not announced as a slogan. It is visible in attention to small living beings when his teachers arrive. This makes a strong, age-safe storyboard: suffering noticed; truthful instruction heard; bow broken; devotional practice begun; ants protected. Each beat can be linked to the CC Madhya 24 range without invented dialogue.

**Source-bounded expansion — devotional conclusion (CC Madhya 24.273–282).** Mṛgāri receives the Vaiṣṇavas with devotion, and his transformed devotional condition becomes evident. Parvata Muni praises Nārada's influence on his disciple. The conclusion places the emphasis on saintly association and devotional transformation, not on Mṛgāri building a public reputation. Claims that travelers sought his blessings, that the forest became famous for protection, or that he later remained humble about his past are not required and are not presented here as facts from this passage.

The conclusion also keeps Nārada's role in view. Nārada sees what others pass by, speaks truth, gives an actionable instruction, addresses the livelihood concern, supplies positive practice, and returns with another Vaiṣṇava to see the disciple. Nothing in that sequence permits a facilitator to claim that every difficult habit will change instantly or that one conversation guarantees sainthood. The story displays what authorized association can awaken when instruction is received and followed.

**Connection to the locked primary verse.** Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 11.9.29 remains the primary verse for C1-W4. CC Madhya 24 supplies the week's kathā; it does not replace the verse chain. The two sources serve different functions. SB 11.9.29 states the rare, temporary, value-giving opportunity of human life. The Mṛgāri account shows that opportunity being used: hearing, questioning an inherited pattern, accepting responsibility, changing conduct, and taking up devotional practice.

“Rare and valuable” should therefore never become a ranking exercise in which children learn contempt for animals or anxiety about their worth. Nārada's compassion for animals is part of the narrative's moral force. Human capacity is presented as responsibility: the ability to receive instruction and choose service rather than refine harm. The week's urgency is calm and practical. Because time matters, protect a small appointment for hearing. Because choices matter, take one supported step. Because beings matter, let devotion become visible as care.

**Age-track delivery.** For Lāla–Lālī, keep four source-grounded sentences: Nārada saw animals suffering; he taught the hunter to stop; the hunter broke his bow and learned devotional practice; later he carefully protected ants in his path. Invite children to point to the “careful step” picture and name one gentle action. Do not ask them to imagine punishment or graphic injury.

For Kiśora–Kiśorī, use the sequence to examine inherited habits without identity shaming. Ask: What changed Mṛgāri's understanding? What practical concern arose when he was told to stop? How did positive practice and community support help make the change sustainable? Youth should be free to discuss a fictional case rather than disclose personal conduct. The source is a lens for ethical reasoning and devotional responsibility, not a tool for public confession.

For parents, emphasize the difference between command and formation. Nārada gave a clear boundary, but he also addressed food, place, practice, association, and follow-up. A parent cannot reproduce Nārada's spiritual authority, yet can learn from the pattern: state harm clearly, make the next action concrete, and provide appropriate support. If a problem involves abuse, addiction, mental health, or danger, the application includes qualified professional and safeguarding help; a kathā discussion is not a substitute.

**Source-integrity pause.** Before narrating, the facilitator should open the chapter link and the verse-level anchors in the source register. If a remembered detail cannot be located in CC Madhya 24.229–282, leave it out or label it for later review. Do not move Dhruva's SB 4.8–4.9 source into this week, and do not borrow Ajāmila's SB 6.1–6.2 source. Keeping those narratives distinct is part of serving families with reliable śāstric guidance.
