## Core teaching script (10–20 minutes — speak, do not summarize only)

### Beat 1 — Decorating the cell (2–3 min)

Invite the room to picture a prisoner who paints the walls, hangs posters, upgrades the bed, and rearranges the desk—yet never asks how to leave. The cell becomes prettier. The bars remain.

Say clearly: “The analogy is about **misplaced ultimate investment**—polishing temporary arrangements while neglecting the door of shelter. It is **not** hatred of a home. Your house is not a prison because it has curtains. Curtains can serve a family. Bondage begins when temporary things become the *ultimate* project and Kṛṣṇa becomes optional decoration.”

Connect to last week briefly: the modes (C2-W4) are how material nature colors experience; this week names the covering energy itself—*māyā*—as hard to cross without the Lord’s help.

### Beat 2 — Primary verse layers (3–4 min)

Read or display Devanāgarī and IAST briefly. State the KUTUMBA teaching meaning again. Emphasize load-bearing phrases from the teaching:

- **Divine / of the Lord (*daivī* / *mama māyā*)** — māyā is Kṛṣṇa’s energy, not an equal rival power. BG 9.10 supports this: nature works under His superintendence.
- **Made of the modes (*guṇa-mayī*)** — continuity with C2-W4; the covering is mode-woven, not random.
- **Hard to overcome (*duratyayā*)** — humility: willpower alone is not the boast; unaided souls do not casually “beat” māyā.
- **Those who surrender (*mām eva ye prapadyante*)** — the crossing is shelter in the Lord, not contempt for matter or people.
- **They cross (*taranti*)** — hope: crossing is real; despair is not devotion.

Clarify carefully: *illusory* in teaching language does **not** mean “the world is fake/evil and duties are nonsense.” It means the covering misdirects the eternal soul’s priorities—making temporary things feel ultimate.

### Beat 3 — Attention cascade (BG 2.62–2.63) (2–3 min)

Paraphrase without purport dump:

- Contemplating sense objects → attachment → lust/craving → anger (BG 2.62).
- Anger → delusion → memory bewilderment → intelligence loss → fall (BG 2.63).

Classroom picture: a ten-second notification becomes twenty minutes of lost Gītā reading—not because phones are demons, but because contemplation without shelter feeds the cascade. Bridge to lab: Attention Boundary and Japa Shelter.

Also name BG 15.7 in one sentence: the eternal fragmental part of the Lord struggles hard with mind and senses—so we speak with compassion, not mockery.

### Beat 4 — Hard misconception blocks (2–3 min)

State aloud, slowly:

1. **Women are not māyā.** Māyā is the Lord’s material energy. Reducing women to “māyā” is a misuse of scripture language and a pastoral failure. Correct it immediately if it appears.
2. **The world is not taught as fake/evil.** Material nature is real as energy under Kṛṣṇa’s direction (BG 9.10). Temporary ≠ worthless for service and duty.
3. **Householder duties are not contemptible.** Feeding children, earning honestly, maintaining a clean home can be offered in devotion; scorning them is not “advanced.”
4. **No addiction confession pressure.** Families may privately choose boundaries; circle time is not a forced testimony night.

### Beat 5 — Crossing: surrender, association, practice (2 min)

Crossing māyā, per BG 7.14’s teaching sense, is surrender to the Lord—expressed for families as: turning toward Kṛṣṇa in prayer and chanting, seeking sādhu association, and small steady practices (japa shelter, media boundary). Do not claim anyone present has “already crossed.” Claim the principle and one practice step.

Name association gently: good company protects attention; isolation with endless stimulation often strengthens the cascade. Practice is not a substitute for surrender—it is how surrender takes a household shape this week.

### Beat 6 — Bridge to lab and home (1–2 min)

Preview **Attention Boundary and Japa Shelter**: choose a short window, silence or park one device/channel, sit, chant a brief attentive round or family response chant. Home minimum: one media or consumption boundary practiced for three days (not forever perfection).

Optional habits-lane sentence (only if asked): “Some research on interruptions can motivate quieter setups; our teaching that māyā is hard and crossed by surrender rests on Bhagavad-gītā 7.14, not on a lab paper.”

## Analogies with limits (≥3)

### 1) Decorating the prison cell

- **Value:** Makes “misplaced ultimate investment” vivid for adults and older students; matches classic teaching imagery used carefully in tradition.
- **Limit:** Analogy ≠ hatred of home. A decorated home can be a place of *bhakti*. Do not tell children “your house is a jail.” Use “if the temporary becomes our only project, we forget the door.”

### 2) Ten-second notification → twenty minutes

- **Value:** Embodies BG 2.62–2.63’s cascade in modern attention life; motivates Attention Boundary without demonizing tools.
- **Limit:** Interruption research (if mentioned) does **not** prove māyā ontology. Phones are not māyā incarnate; misuse of attention is the pedagogical target.

### 3) Beautiful cage

- **Value:** Comfort and beauty can coexist with bondage when the door is ignored; helps adults notice “upgrade as ultimate project.”
- **Limit:** Beauty and comfort are not sins. Do not shame families for renovations, gifts, or aesthetics. The issue is *ultimate* priority and forgetfulness of the Lord—not paint color.

## Three constructed family cases

### Case 1 — Phone while reading Gītā

- **Concrete situation:** A parent opens Bhagavad-gītā after dinner. A notification arrives. Ten seconds become twenty minutes of scrolling. The verse is unread. Frustration rises; someone snaps at a child for “interrupting,” though the phone interrupted first.
- **Mistaken conclusion:** “I just need more willpower—or else phones are evil and we should hate technology.”
- **Principle:** Contemplation of sense objects feeds attachment and cascade (BG 2.62–2.63); māyā’s covering is hard (BG 7.14)—so design a boundary and take shelter in japa/reading, not contempt for tools or people.
- **Source:** BG 7.14; BG 2.62; BG 2.63 — https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/14/
- **Compassionate response:** “Attention slipped. Let’s park the phone and read one verse together.”
- **Action:** Attention Boundary and Japa Shelter: device face-down in another room (or Do Not Disturb), three minutes chant or one verse aloud.
- **What not to say:** “You’re so māyā.” “Real devotees smash their phones.” Forced confession of screen hours in circle.
- **Optional age adaptation:** Younger—practice “phone sleeps in the basket” game. Older—write one boundary rule and one shelter phrase.

### Case 2 — House upgrade as ultimate project

- **Concrete situation:** For months, family conversation centers on renovations, finishes, comparisons with neighbors, and shopping lists. Temple visits and family chanting shrink “until the project is done.” Someone jokes that spiritual life can wait until the kitchen is perfect.
- **Mistaken conclusion:** “Improving the house is worldly—so householders can’t be spiritual,” OR the reverse: “The upgrade *is* our spiritual life now.”
- **Principle:** Decorating bondage means treating temporary arrangements as ultimate while delaying shelter (prison-cell analogy with limit). BG 9.10 keeps nature under the Lord’s direction—use matter in service, don’t enthrone it. BG 7.14: surrender is the crossing.
- **Source:** BG 7.14; BG 9.10 — https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/14/
- **Compassionate response:** “Homes can serve families. Let’s not postpone the holy name until the last tile.”
- **Action:** Keep one short daily shelter practice during the project week; one consumption boundary (e.g., no upgrade-browsing after 8 p.m. for three days).
- **What not to say:** “Your house is a prison.” Shame for honest repair needs. Contempt for grihastha life.
- **Optional age adaptation:** Younger—draw a home *and* a chant corner. Older—list “serve” vs “ultimate” uses of a home project.

### Case 3 — Misuse of “māyā” toward women (correct explicitly)

- **Concrete situation:** In a side conversation after class, someone says a woman relative is “total māyā” because she dresses nicely or because a young man felt attracted. Another adult laughs along. A teenage girl overhears.
- **Mistaken conclusion:** “Scripture teaches that women *are* māyā.”
- **Principle:** BG 7.14 names *māyā* as the Lord’s divine material energy made of the modes—hard to overcome, crossed by surrender—not a slur for women. Attraction and sense control are personal responsibility themes (see BG 2.62 cascade), not licenses to demean persons. Living beings are eternal parts of the Lord (BG 15.7).
- **Source:** BG 7.14; BG 15.7; BG 2.62 — https://vedabase.io/en/library/bg/7/14/
- **Compassionate response:** “Stop. Women are not māyā. Māyā is the Lord’s covering energy. We speak respectfully about people.”
- **Action:** Facilitator models public correction without shaming the overheard girl; privately coach the speaker if needed; reinforce speech ethics in parent circle.
- **What not to say:** Anything that equates women’s existence/appearance with illusory energy as identity. Jokes that reduce mothers, sisters, or classmates to “temptation objects.”
- **Optional age adaptation:** Younger—practice kind words about friends. Older—write one sentence distinguishing energy/covering from person-respect.

## Five likely questions with source-based answers

**Q1: Is māyā scientifically proven?**  
**A:** Māyā as the Lord’s mode-made material energy and the path of surrender are taught by śāstra (BG 7.14). Empirical studies may describe attention interruption and stress in modern media environments, but they do **not** prove or disprove māyā ontology. Keep doctrine on scripture; keep science (if used) in the habits lane only.

**Q2: Should we hate material life and leave household duties?**  
**A:** No. This week blocks contempt for householder duties. Material nature operates under the Lord’s direction (BG 9.10). Temporary arrangements can be used in service; the warning is against making them ultimate while refusing shelter (BG 7.14).

**Q3: Aren’t women called māyā in some talks I’ve heard?**  
**A:** Kutumba’s week teaching explicitly rejects equating women with māyā. BG 7.14 addresses the Lord’s illusory material energy. Persons—women and men—are living beings, eternal fragmental parts of the Lord (BG 15.7). Correct the misuse immediately.

**Q4: If māyā is hard to overcome, why bother with small boundaries?**  
**A:** Small boundaries are expressions of turning toward shelter—not boasts that we defeated māyā by technique. BG 7.14 centers surrender; Attention Boundary and Japa Shelter are humble practices that support turning to the Lord, association, and chanting.

**Q5: Do we have to confess our screen addictions in class?**  
**A:** No. Privacy protects dignity. Offer a voluntary three-day boundary; allow pass; never fish for addiction testimony. Defer clinical addiction or crisis needs to qualified help outside class.

**Q6: Is the prison-cell analogy saying my home is bad?**  
**A:** No. The analogy targets misplaced *ultimate* investment. A home can be a place of service, affection, and chanting. The limit is explicit: analogy ≠ hatred of home.

**Q7: How does this connect to last week’s modes?**  
**A:** C2-W4 taught that the modes condition the embodied self. BG 7.14 names māyā as *guṇa-mayī*—mode-made—and hard to cross without surrender. Modes describe coloring; māyā names the covering energy that is difficult to overcome.

**Q8: What if our three-day boundary fails on day two?**  
**A:** Restart kindly without public shame. The point is turning toward shelter, not performing perfection. Adjust the boundary to something smaller and keepable.
